## 1. STRUCTURAL MOATS BLOCKING AFFORDABLE HOUSING CONSTRUCTION

### 1.1 Zoning/land-use incumbency

San Mateo County sits inside a region where exclusionary zoning is the default, not the exception. A UC Berkeley Othering & Belonging Institute study found roughly 85% of *residential* land across the San Francisco Bay Area is reserved exclusively for single-family housing (Source: Othering & Belonging Institute, https://belonging.berkeley.edu/uc-berkeley-study-staggering-96-california-residential-land-zoned-single-family-housing).

Within the county:

- **Unincorporated San Mateo County**: ~75% of the urban unincorporated area is covered by three single-family-only zoning districts — R-1/S-73 (North Fair Oaks), R-1/S-17 (Midcoast), and the Residential Hillside District (Source: County of San Mateo Planning, https://www.smcgov.org/planning/san-mateo-county-housing-element-update).
- **Menlo Park**: R-1-S lots require a 10,000 sq ft minimum with 20 ft front/rear and 10-12 ft side setbacks (Source: Menlo Park Municipal Code Ch. 16.16, https://www.codepublishing.com/CA/MenloPark/html/MenloPark16/MenloPark1616.html).
- **Belmont**: Slope-based FAR caps (0.533 on flat lots), gross floor area capped at 3,500-4,500 sq ft, height capped at 28 ft in R-1 zones (Source: GoCodebook, https://gocodebook.com/us/california/belmont/development-standards).
- **Daly City**: Multifamily exterior modifications trigger mandatory design review (Ch. 17.45); the Planning Commission recently extended permit validity to 5 years (Source: Citizen Portal / Daly City Municipal Code, https://citizenportal.ai/articles/7619983/california/san-mateo-county/daly-city/daly-city-planning-panel-backs-minor-zoning-code-changes-extends-permit-life-to-five-years).
- **South San Francisco**: Height above base limits requires a discretionary Use Permit; added multifamily floor area requires a Conditional Use Permit (Source: SSF Municipal Code Ch. 20.350, https://ecode360.com/43452068).

San Mateo County is characterized as "least compliant in the Bay Area" with state housing mandates and carries a **17:1 jobs-to-housing ratio — the worst imbalance in the Bay Area** (Source: ABC7 News, https://abc7news.com/post/fight-build-housing-san-mateo-county-least-compliant/14976592/). **[Updated Sept 2026]** Independent fact-check found this 17:1 figure traces to a 2010-2015/2016-period stat (72,000 new jobs vs. ~3,800 new homes) that has circulated in local media since ~2018 and continues to be recycled by ABC7 and others as if current — no county-wide jobs-housing ratio has been independently recomputed for 2024-2026. Treat 17:1 as a frequently-cited *historical* statistic illustrating the scale of the imbalance, not a live current measurement. See Verification Log below.

### 1.2 CEQA litigation as a delay/veto tool

- CEQA review adds an estimated **6-12 months** per infill project (Source: Ben Metcalf, UC Berkeley Terner Center, via CalMatters, https://calmatters.org/housing/2025/06/ceqa-urban-development-infill-budget/).
- Former Governor Jerry Brown: construction unions use CEQA "as a hammer to get project labor agreements" — filing or threatening CEQA appeals, dropped in exchange for union-labor concessions (Source: San Jose Inside, https://www.sanjoseinside.com/news/ceqa-racket-extracts-11th-hour-payoffs-amidst-housing-crisis/).
- **Redwood City**: "Redwood City Residents for Responsible Development" used environmental litigation to force an 8-story, 91-unit downtown condo proposal (603 Jefferson Ave) down to 6 stories/68 units — a loss of 23 homes via settlement (Source: Bay Area Metropolitan Observer, http://sfbamo.com/news/lawsuit-eliminates-23-homes-from-redwood-city-condo-proposal/).
- CEQA used even against **nonprofit affordable housing** — a Habitat for Humanity 20-home project in the county was delayed via CEQA challenge (Source: San Mateo Daily Journal, https://www.smdailyjournal.com/opinion/guest_perspectives/ceqa-abuse-delays-frustrates-affordable-housing/article_4f04b844-951c-11e8-bfac-4759a0b619fd.html).
- **2025 state reform** exempts most urban infill housing from CEQA review entirely (Source: CalMatters, https://calmatters.org/housing/2025/06/ceqa-urban-development-infill-budget/). Opponents are pivoting to ballot-box tactics instead (see 1.3).

### 1.3 Local discretionary review / "NIMBY veto" structures

- **San Mateo (city) Measure T (Nov. 2024)**: because the adopted General Plan 2040 exceeded height/density caps from a prior voter-approved measure (Measure Y, 2020), state law required a *second* ballot measure to legalize the upzoning needed to meet RHNA obligations. Passed, but shows upzoning in San Mateo runs through the ballot box, not just the council (Source: City of San Mateo, https://www.cityofsanmateo.org/ballotmeasure).
- **Menlo Park anti-housing ballot measure (qualified 2025, ballot Nov. 2026)**: would require voter approval before any change of use — including surplus-land disposal — for three city-owned parking lots, retroactively blocking dispositions after May 15, 2025, directly threatening 345+ planned affordable homes, with the city funding proponents' legal defense if challenged. HLC of San Mateo County calls this the new NIMBY "playbook" (Source: https://hlcsmc.org/2025/11/03/anti-housing-ballot-measure-in-menlo-park-becomes-new-nimby-playbook/). **[Updated Sept 2026]** Corrected date/status: this originated as a 2025 petition drive but the measure has since qualified for the **November 2026** ballot (qualified Oct 21, per The Almanac / Davis Vanguard reporting) — the vote itself is still pending as of this report's update, not a completed 2025 event as the original "(2025)" label implied.
- Mandatory discretionary layers persist county-wide: Design Review Permits and Use Permits in unincorporated areas; mandatory multifamily design review in Daly City; CUPs for added density/height in South San Francisco.
- HLC of San Mateo County has found city commissions contradict their own adopted Housing Element commitments during discretionary review of individual projects (https://hlcsmc.org/2024/05/09/housing-element-review-city-commissions-contradict-housing-element-commitments/).
- Litigation cuts both ways: Housing Action Coalition sued San Mateo's 2023-2031 Housing Element within 24 hours of adoption, alleging over half its low-income obligation relied on non-viable sites (Source: SF Standard, https://sfstandard.com/2024/05/28/san-mateo-housing-element-meets-yimby-challenge/).

### 1.5 Land cost capture

- Midpen + Peninsula Open Space Trust purchased the 6,300-acre Cloverdale Ranch, permanently removing it from the buildable pool (San Mateo Daily Journal).
- Woodside alone had ~4,000 acres of large-lot/ranch land listed (~$331M combined) — land wealth concentrated in low-density estate holdings.
- 12 "mega landlords" (Veritas, Invitation Homes, Greystar, Equity Residential, Essex, UDR, AvalonBay, Tricon, et al.) own a majority of Bay Area rental stock (Fast Company).
- Homeowner-dominated local political participation (older, higher-equity, high planning-meeting turnout) is reported as a direct driver of the county's jobs-housing imbalance (ABC7, SFGate).

### 1.6 Cui bono — who benefits from the status quo

- **Incumbent homeowners** — zoning scarcity drives most of their net-worth appreciation; highest-turnout local voting bloc.
- **Existing landlords / institutional owners** — scarcity supports rents without build risk.
- **Construction trade unions** — CEQA/discretionary review leveraged for PLA concessions, independent of environmental merit.
- **Fiscally constrained cities** — Prop 13 "fiscalization of land use": commercial/sales tax beats housing's net fiscal cost.
- **Large low-density landowners** — capture scarcity value without development risk.
- **Some employers** (two-sided) — a constrained workforce can suppress wage competition in some framings, but cuts against employers facing housing-driven attrition in hard-to-fill roles (nursing, teaching).

## 2. STATE LAW OVERRIDE / PREEMPTION LAYER

- **HAA (1982, strengthened 2017+; AB 1893, 2024)** — limits denial/downsizing of compliant projects; eligibility locks at application-completeness date; private right of action + mandatory attorney's fees. **CARLA v. City of San Mateo**: First District Court of Appeal ruled the city's subjective-design-standard denial of a 10-unit project violated HAA; city settled for **$450,000** — a statewide landmark precedent originating in this county.
- **SB 35 (2017)** — streamlined ministerial approval (≤6 months, no CEQA/discretionary hearing) for qualifying infill in jurisdictions missing RHNA benchmarks (county sits in the 20% affordable-threshold tier). No specific SB 35 project confirmed filed in the county in this pass — Builder's Remedy dominates local streamlining activity instead.
- **SB 9 (2021)** — by-right lot splits + up to 2 units/parcel (≤4 units total). City of San Mateo adopted a Two-Unit Development Overlay ordinance; unincorporated county applies ministerial review with owner-occupancy affidavit.
- **SB 10 (2021)** — local-option up to 10 units/parcel near transit, CEQA-exempt for the zoning action. City of San Mateo has mapped qualifying transit geography (Caltrain stations, El Camino Real corridor) but **no jurisdiction in the county has actually adopted SB 10** — eligibility exists, uptake is zero.
- **Builder's Remedy** — loss of zoning-based denial power for jurisdictions lacking a certified Housing Element. **8 of the county's jurisdictions were non-compliant as of Sept 2024** (Pacifica, Half Moon Bay, Daly City, San Bruno, Belmont, Atherton, Woodside, unincorporated county) — **highest non-compliance rate in the Bay Area (38% vs. 19% regional)**. **[Updated Sept 2026]** The aggregate 38%-vs-19% rate is independently confirmed, but the specific 8-jurisdiction roster could not be pinned to a single current independent source — compliance status is a moving target (several jurisdictions have since certified or been sued into compliance). Treat the named list as an approximately-correct Sept-2024 snapshot, not a permanently current roster; see Verification Log. Pacifica: 6+ Builder's Remedy projects (incl. the Pacifica Quarry proposal — **[Updated Sept 2026]** grown to **1,225 units** (Coastal Crest Residences) and **rejected 5-0 by Pacifica's Planning Commission on Aug 3 (2026)**, which argued state housing law does not override the city's voter-approved land-use requirement; this is now a contested, active legal fight over whether Builder's Remedy overrides local control, not a settled pipeline success — see Verification Log). Menlo Park: 3 Builder's Remedy applications filed pre-certification, including an 80 Willow Road 300-ft tower proposal and a Willow-Sunset project (665 units, 3 towers up to 37 stories, 20% affordable).
- **RHNA 6th Cycle (2023-2031)**: county total **47,687 units** (vs. 16,340 in 5th cycle). Largest allocations: San Mateo city 7,015; Daly City 4,838; Redwood City 4,588; South San Francisco 3,956; Menlo Park 2,946; unincorporated county 2,833.

## 3. MARKET-RATE AFFORDABILITY PROBLEM

**Prices/rents**: County median sale price ~$1.6M (Redfin, Nov 2025); Menlo Park $3.3M, Redwood City $1.9M. Market 2BR rent ~$3,979-$4,289/mo (city of San Mateo, Zumper/RentCafe).

**HUD figures (FY2025, SF HUD Metro FMR Area)**: 4-person AMI **$186,600**; 80% AMI ("low income") threshold **$156,650** — meaning a household earning the county's actual median income is formally "low income" by HUD's own standard. FMR 2BR: $3,318/mo.

**Income required** (30%-of-income rule, 2BR):
- At market rent (~$4,050/mo): **~$162,000/yr**
- At HUD FMR ($3,318/mo): **~$132,720/yr**

**Income required to buy** (median $1.6M home, 20% down, 30-yr fixed ~6.75%, PITI ≈ $9,900-$10,200/mo):
- At 28% DTI: **~$424,000/yr**
- At 36% DTI: **~$330,000/yr**

**Actual median household income, San Mateo County**: **~$158,900** (Census ACS; 2nd-highest of any CA county).

| Requirement | Income needed | Actual median | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Afford 2BR at HUD FMR | ~$132,720 | ~$158,900 | median clears it, barely |
| Afford 2BR at market rent | ~$162,000 | ~$158,900 | median falls just short |
| Buy median home (36% DTI) | ~$330,000 | ~$158,900 | needs **~2.1x** actual income |
| Buy median home (28% DTI) | ~$424,000 | ~$158,900 | needs **~2.7x** actual income |

Even the county's median household income (2nd-highest in CA) falls $170K-$265K/yr short of affording the median home — ownership is effectively reachable only via existing equity, inherited wealth, or dual-high-earner tech/finance households.

**Workforce wages vs. requirement**:
- Teachers (SMFCSD, BA Step 1): **$70,977/yr**; SMUHSD experienced range up to ~$113,761 — well under the ~$132,720-$162,000 rent threshold, ~1/3 of home-purchase requirement.
- RNs (SF-Oakland-Fremont metro): ~$148,330-$165,344/yr — the one occupation that clears 2BR rent affordability alone, still far short of purchase affordability.
- Retail workers: national median ~$35,400/yr — far below rent affordability on a single income.
- Food service: SF metro mean ~$46,000-$47,650/yr — roughly a third of the 2BR market-rent threshold.

**Bottom line**: teachers, retail, and food-service workers cannot afford median 2BR rent on a single income at the 30% standard; nurses are the outlier. This is the direct wage-side mechanism by which Section 1's structural moats produce the county's public-service/service-economy staffing crisis.

## 4. SYNTHESIS — CUI BONO AND RANKED INTERVENTIONS (Pareto)

### Who structurally benefits (follow the money/control)
1. **Incumbent single-family homeowners** — largest, most durable beneficiary; scarcity drives their net worth; dominant local voter/hearing turnout; now exporting control via ballot-box mechanisms (Menlo Park pattern).
2. **Construction trade unions** — CEQA/review as PLA leverage, independent of project merits.
3. **Existing landlords / institutional multifamily owners** — scarcity-supported rents, no build-risk exposure.
4. **Large low-density landowners** (Woodside, Portola Valley, hillside estates) — scarcity-premium capture, reinforced by open-space acquisition removing adjacent buildable land.
5. **Fiscally constrained municipalities** — Prop 13 fiscalization-of-land-use bias toward commercial over housing entitlement.
6. **State-level actors (HCD, legislature, HAC, CARLA)** — the counter-force; San Mateo County is one of the most contested arenas in the state (highest non-compliance rate, an HAA landmark case, active Builder's Remedy pipeline, a live 2025 NIMBY ballot countermove).

### Ranked interventions — impact per unit of effort, highest to lowest

1. **Close the ballot-box CEQA-replacement loophole** (highest leverage, lowest marginal cost) — state preemption barring referendum-style blocks on surplus-land/affordable-housing site decisions (the Menlo Park pattern), before it proliferates county-wide. The 2025 CEQA infill exemption already removed the largest historical veto tool; this closes the newest one.
2. **Aggressive Builder's Remedy enforcement in the 8 non-compliant SMC jurisdictions** — moderate effort (advocacy/litigation capacity already exists via HLC/CA YIMBY), very high impact — bypasses the entire local zoning/discretionary stack at once (Pacifica, Menlo Park already show thousand-unit-scale pipelines proposed). **[Updated Sept 2026]** Pacifica Quarry, the anchor example for this claim, was rejected 5-0 by Pacifica's Planning Commission on Aug 3 (2026) on the argument that state law does not override the city's voter-approved land-use requirement — this is now an open legal contest over whether Builder's Remedy actually forces approval, not a proven pipeline outcome; the intervention's impact should be read as "high if enforcement litigation succeeds," not as already-demonstrated.
3. **Systematic HAA private-right-of-action litigation** — the CARLA precedent model; legal-cost-only, no construction capital; outsized deterrent value via fee-shifting liability.
4. **Mandatory SB 10 adoption on already-mapped transit corridors** — zero jurisdictions have used a tool whose eligibility geography is already analyzed; low-to-moderate effort (no new environmental/engineering work), high impact.
5. **Fiscal reform ending the sales-tax/commercial bias (fiscalization of land use)** — highest durability (targets the *incentive*, not just the veto tools built on it) but highest effort (state fiscal/tax policy change).
6. **Impact-fee rationalization on 5-19 unit projects** — low effort (ordinance change), moderate impact, smaller scope than the above.
7. **Direct upzoning of large low-density landholdings + open-space acquisition reform** — lowest-ranked: hardest political resistance (concentrated wealth), slowest-to-realize payoff despite large raw acreage.

**Pattern**: the two highest-leverage moves attack the *veto machinery* directly and require legal/political effort, not capital. The lowest-leverage move confronts land-value concentration at its most politically entrenched point. San Mateo County is a live natural experiment: it simultaneously holds the state's highest Builder's Remedy exposure, its most-cited HAA precedent, and its newest NIMBY ballot-measure countermove.

This corroborates and extends Section 3's finding: essential-workforce wages across public safety, government, childcare, and construction are broadly outmatched by both rental and ownership costs at standard affordability ratios, not just the teacher/nurse/retail/food-service set originally sampled.
